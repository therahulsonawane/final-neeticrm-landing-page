**The problem:** your ad dashboard says cost per lead is down 20 per cent, yet bookings are flat. Money is going into the channels that look cheapest, and sales say the leads are poor.

If cheaper leads do not pick up, visit or book, you have not saved money. You have bought more noise. The question is: **what does a booking cost, and from which source?**

## An example

The figures below are illustrative, not real customer results.

| | Source A | Source B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spend | ₹1,00,000 | ₹1,00,000 |
| Leads | 400 | 150 |
| Cost per lead | ₹250 | ₹667 |
| Site visits | 12 | 20 |
| Cost per site visit | ₹8,333 | ₹5,000 |
| Bookings | 1 | 3 |
| Cost per booking | ₹1,00,000 | ₹33,333 |

Source A wins on cost per lead and loses badly on cost per booking. Moving budget by lead cost alone moves it the wrong way.

## Why you cannot see this today

You need three things joined: spend by source, which leads came from each source, and which of them booked. Most businesses keep these in the ad account, a lead sheet and a bookings register, so nobody compares them.

## The solution: track four costs

1. **Cost per lead:** useful for volume and creatives, never the whole story.
2. **Cost per qualified lead:** a salesperson confirmed budget, timeline and interest.
3. **Cost per site visit:** a real buyer reached the project.
4. **Cost per booking:** the outcome that matters.

Use early metrics for quick decisions and booking cost for big budget calls.

## How to set it up

**Tag every lead** with source, campaign and project, using consistent names ("Facebook", not "FB").

**Record spend weekly** by source and project.

**Define stages clearly:** qualified, visit scheduled, visit completed, booked, with a booking marked only on proof such as a token.

**Review by cohort.** Check leads from a given month after 30, 60 and 90 days instead of judging a campaign after a week.

**Decide:** scale sources with a low booking cost, fix those with many leads but few visits, and stop those that miss on every measure after a fair test.

## Feed quality back to ad platforms

Ad algorithms optimise for what you tell them is a win. When your CRM can report which leads were qualified, visited or booked, supported integrations can help campaigns optimise toward better buyers. This depends on the platform and your setup, so check what your account supports.

## Check the sales side before cutting a source

A high booking cost is not always a marketing problem. Late calls and inconsistent follow-up ruin good leads, so read [how to stop lead leakage](/blog/real-estate-lead-leakage/) first. Compare salespeople as well: if one converts the same leads into visits at twice the rate, that is coaching, not budget.

## Weekly review checklist

- Leads, qualified leads, visits and bookings by source
- Cost at each step
- Response time by source
- Top reasons for lost leads
- One decision to change this week

## A note on small numbers

Real estate bookings are few, so one extra booking can swing a source's cost per booking dramatically. Do not overreact to a single month. Look at a rolling three-month view, and use cost per qualified lead and cost per site visit as earlier indicators while bookings catch up. When two sources are close, keep testing both rather than declaring a winner too early.

## Questions this report should answer

- Which source gives the cheapest qualified leads, and which gives the cheapest visits?
- Which project is costing the most per booking, and why?
- Where do leads from a source drop off: before the first call, before the visit or after it?
- Are follow-up and response times different by source?

If you can answer these every week, your budget decisions stop being guesses.

## Do not forget the other costs

Marketing spend is only part of what a booking costs. Brokerage, telecaller salaries and tools all add up. Start with marketing cost per booking because it is easiest to measure and compare, then widen the view once the basics are working.
